# Indonesian, English, and transcreation

Use this reference to choose a real register and preserve meaning across languages. Correct grammar is a baseline; natural copy also needs the audience’s vocabulary, relationship, channel, and decision context.

## Calibrate the voice

Use three to five representative brand samples when available. Extract observable traits rather than vague adjectives:

- pronouns and relationship to the reader;
- formal, neutral, or colloquial register;
- typical sentence and paragraph length;
- common verbs, nouns, product terms, and contractions;
- punctuation, capitalization, humor, and directness;
- English–Indonesian switching and which concepts trigger it;
- emoji types, frequency, position, and purpose;
- phrases the brand never uses.

Weight recent, approved, high-performing, and channel-matched samples more heavily. Customer reviews, interviews, support messages, search queries, and sales-call language can reveal how the audience describes the problem. Remove personal data and do not present a paraphrase as a direct quote.

When no samples exist, choose one explicit register and keep it stable. Do not synthesize “human” voice by adding random slang.

## Write Indonesian copy

### Choose the relationship

Use one pronoun system deliberately:

- `saya/Anda`: formal or neutral individual address. Capitalize `Anda` under EYD.
- `kami/Anda`: a business speaking to an individual or organization. `Kami` excludes the reader.
- `kita`: includes the reader. Use only when the action or group genuinely includes both brand and audience.
- `aku/kamu`: familiar and personal. Use when the brand and audience support that closeness.
- `gue/lo`: strongly informal and regionally marked. Never use as a default for national Indonesian.

Do not drift from `Anda` to `kamu`, or from `kami` to `kita`, unless the relationship actually changes.

### Distinguish a voice choice from an error

Use EYD V and KBBI as the baseline for formal copy. Conversational copy may use spoken forms deliberately, but keep the deviation consistent with the voice.

- Formal: `Periksa biaya dan risikonya sebelum mengajukan pembiayaan.`
- Conversational: `Cek biayanya dulu, baru putuskan.`
- Uncontrolled: formal opening, random `bestie`, mixed pronouns, and accidental spelling changes.

Check common high-visibility details:

- write the prepositions `di` and `ke` separately from a place; join the passive prefix `di-` to its verb;
- use `Anda` with a capital letter;
- format dates, currency, percentages, and ranges consistently;
- apply punctuation for comprehension, not decoration;
- use a term consistently instead of cycling through synonyms.

Do not force formal spelling onto a quoted slogan, product name, or intentionally colloquial voice. Do not use EYD as an excuse for stiff bureaucratic prose.

### Remove translation-shaped Indonesian

Prefer the construction an Indonesian speaker would choose:

- `melakukan pembelian` → `membeli` or `beli`;
- `memiliki kemampuan untuk membantu meningkatkan` → `membantu meningkatkan`;
- `potong pekerjaan sibuk` → `kurangi kerja admin`;
- `dapatkan bayaran lebih cepat` → state the actual mechanism, such as `kirim invoice lebih cepat dan pantau status pembayarannya`;
- `Apakah Anda siap untuk membawa bisnis ke level berikutnya?` → name the real next outcome;
- `hadir sebagai solusi` → say what the product does.

Prefer a concrete customer situation over a broad category opening. Replace `Di era digital yang serba cepat` with the moment that creates the need.

### Use colloquial markers only when earned

Words and particles such as `nih`, `kok`, `deh`, `banget`, `nggak`, and `-mu` signal relationship, place, and age. Match observed usage. Do not sprinkle them into otherwise formal copy.

Avoid automatic pseudo-closeness such as `bestie`, `sobat`, `Bunda`, `Bos`, or `Kak` without audience and brand evidence.

### Write Indonesian CTAs as real next steps

Use the action that follows:

- `Lihat demo`
- `Bandingkan paket`
- `Cek area pengiriman`
- `Tanya stok`
- `Coba gratis` only when the offer is genuinely free and its terms are clear
- `Ajukan simulasi` only when the next screen actually provides one

Avoid `Klik di sini`, `Buruan!!!`, and repeated `Yuk` when they add no information.

### Watch for Indonesian slop clusters

Treat these as prompts to inspect, not banned strings:

- `Di era digital yang serba cepat...`
- `Hadir sebagai solusi inovatif...`
- `Bukan sekadar X, tetapi Y.`
- `Bawa bisnis Anda ke level berikutnya.`
- `Wujudkan impian Anda.`
- `Rasakan pengalaman...`
- `Aman, mudah, dan terpercaya.`
- `Dirancang khusus untuk...`
- `Kini saatnya...`
- `Jangan lewatkan kesempatan emas.`

Keep one only when the surrounding detail, evidence, and voice make it specific to this brand.

## Write English copy

### Lock the locale

Choose `en-US`, `en-GB`, or another target. Keep spelling, dates, currency, measurements, punctuation, and legal terms consistent. Do not mix `color` with `optimise`.

For global English:

- prefer familiar words and short dependency chains;
- explain uncommon acronyms on first use;
- avoid culture-bound idioms, puns, sports metaphors, and humor unless localization is planned;
- keep terminology stable so translation and comprehension remain reliable;
- use contractions when the voice supports them;
- use singular `they` rather than guessing gender.

### Prefer the concrete verb

- `utilize` → `use`;
- `facilitate the creation of` → `create` or name who creates what;
- `provides the ability to track` → `tracks`;
- `designed to empower teams` → state the task the team can now complete;
- `elevate your workflow` → name the work, delay, or error that changes.

Avoid thesaurus variation. Repeating the correct product term is clearer than rotating through elegant synonyms.

### Watch for English slop clusters

Inspect combinations such as:

- `unlock`, `elevate`, `revolutionary`, `seamless`, `empower`, `game-changer`;
- `in today’s fast-paced world`;
- `not just X—it’s Y`;
- `designed to transform`;
- `whether you’re X, Y, or Z`;
- evenly polished groups of three;
- abstract benefits without a mechanism or proof.

A listed word may be correct. Remove it when it carries the persuasion that a fact should carry.

## Handle mixed language

Code-switch only when it serves the audience:

- the audience actually uses the term;
- the product or field has a stable English term;
- the switch communicates an established identity or tone;
- the English word is clearer than an awkward forced translation.

Do not add English as prestige garnish. `Powerful solution untuk growth maksimal` contains no useful message in either language.

Keep one base language per asset unless evidence supports a mixed voice. When introducing a technical term, consider an Indonesian explanation on first use. Do not assume urban, young, or professional audiences all prefer the same mix.

## Transcreate

Preserve these invariants:

1. intended action;
2. central promise;
3. product mechanism;
4. proof and its limitations;
5. offer terms and risk;
6. level of urgency;
7. relationship and brand character.

Rebuild everything else for the target language: sentence order, idiom, rhythm, references, humor, information density, and CTA.

Example:

> Source: `Cut the busywork. See which invoices still need a person.`

Literal but weak:

> `Potong pekerjaan sibuk. Lihat invoice mana yang masih membutuhkan seseorang.`

Natural neutral Indonesian:

> `Kurangi kerja admin. Lihat invoice mana yang masih perlu dicek.`

Natural casual Indonesian:

> `Biar admin nggak makan waktu. Invoice yang masih perlu dicek langsung kelihatan.`

Choose one target register; do not return every register unless the user asks.

Back-check the target without translating it word for word back to the source:

- Does it make the same promise?
- Does it preserve every factual limit and material term?
- Does the relationship feel equally close or formal?
- Does the CTA cause the same next action?
- Does any idiom, joke, or code-switch introduce a new implication?

Never turn nationality into a personality assumption. Broad cultural-value adaptation has small average effects and cannot replace audience research.

## Write inclusively and accessibly

- Do not infer gender, health, income, religion, disability, family role, or other sensitive traits.
- Use Indonesian `dia` or English singular `they` when gender is irrelevant.
- Do not assume every woman is a mother, every man is a provider, or every household has the same structure.
- Avoid pity language and stereotypes about disability. Do not use `normal` to mean nondisabled.
- Avoid metaphors such as `buta`, `tuli`, or `gila` when they demean a condition or person.
- Respect community preference between person-first and identity-first language when known.
- Keep the message understandable without color, image, audio, or emoji.
- Do not reveal sensitive targeting in the copy.

## Final language checks

- Can a native speaker identify the intended relationship immediately?
- Are pronouns, locale, spelling, terminology, and punctuation consistent?
- Would the audience naturally use these words for this problem?
- Is any English or slang present only to manufacture status or friendliness?
- Did transcreation preserve the claim, proof, terms, risk, and CTA?
- Does the copy remain accurate when read literally?
- Did the edit remove generic wording without introducing fake quirkiness?
