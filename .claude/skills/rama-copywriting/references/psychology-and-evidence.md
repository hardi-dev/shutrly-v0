# Psychology and evidence

Use this reference to choose a persuasion approach, not to decorate copy with “psychology tricks.” Consumer effects are usually conditional on audience, product, channel, timing, creative, and outcome.

## Evidence standard

Separate three kinds of guidance:

- **Evidence-backed direction:** supported across field data, experiments, or a relevant meta-analysis. Apply it with its stated boundary.
- **Context-dependent hypothesis:** plausible and supported in some settings. Turn it into a named variant and test it.
- **Drafting heuristic:** useful for organizing thought but not established as a behavioral law.

Never convert an average research effect into a promised commercial lift. Purchase intention is not purchase, a click is not profit, and a laboratory scenario is not a live campaign.

## Principles that travel reasonably well

### Make relevance legible

Let the reader recognize the situation, audience, category, or desired outcome quickly. Simple wording increased attention and clicks across a very large set of headline experiments, but that evidence concerns headline engagement rather than downstream sales.

Apply it by naming the real situation and outcome. Do not reduce a technical claim until it becomes inaccurate.

### Use relevant concrete language

Concrete wording can signal attention and understanding. Field data and experiments found higher customer satisfaction, willingness to purchase, and actual spending when service language was more concrete. The benefit disappeared when the response itself was irrelevant.

Apply it by replacing abstractions with the actual object, action, time, place, mechanism, or example a decision needs. Do not invent a number merely to look specific.

### Reduce avoidable processing effort

Prefer familiar words, stable terminology, readable syntax, visible terms, and a clean information sequence. Difficult presentation can increase choice deferral or reliance on familiar cues. There is no reliable general benefit to making a message harder to process so people will “think deeper.”

Fluency can also make claims feel easier or more familiar than their evidence warrants. Use clarity to expose the truth, not to disguise weak proof.

### Keep message scent

Repeat the core promise accurately across the ad, headline, landing page, and CTA. In one field experiment, verbatim headline wording on a button outperformed a gist or unrelated message. Treat that as a strong default to test, not a universal law.

### Pair emotion with a reason to believe

Emotion helps a consequence feel relevant. Evidence, mechanism, terms, and source credibility help the reader believe and evaluate it. Meta-analytic work finds average differences between appeals, but product involvement, media, year, objective, and execution moderate the result.

For a high-risk or high-involvement choice, favor argument quality, comparison criteria, limitations, and proof. For feed interruption or a low-involvement choice, lead with fast recognition and a credible cue, then make verification easy.

### Preserve autonomy

Freedom-threatening language tends to increase anger, negative thoughts, and psychological reactance, which reduce persuasion. Make the next step explicit without repeatedly commanding, shaming, or cornering the reader.

Use `See the plans`, `Check availability`, or `Try the demo` when those accurately describe the action. Reserve `Buy now` for a genuinely ready, low-ambiguity purchase context rather than applying it by habit.

### Build credibility from inspectable proof

Expertise, trustworthiness, and relevant similarity affect how people evaluate electronic word of mouth. Name the source, show why it is credible, and connect the proof to the exact claim.

A useful limitation can improve credibility in some contexts. Mention a real trade-off when it helps someone choose; do not insert a scripted “confession” to manufacture trust.

## Context-dependent tactics

### Personalization

Personalization is positive on average in a large 2026 meta-analysis, with substantial moderation by the data used and level of personalization. It can also trigger privacy concerns and reactance.

Use volunteered context, current intent, role, or a visible use case. Do not reveal invisible tracking, sensitive inference, or personal facts simply because they are available. Prefer “For finance teams closing monthly reports” over “We saw you reviewed payroll tools at 1:14 a.m.”

### Social proof

Real peer cues and close-tie endorsements can influence ad response. Credibility and similarity matter. Social cues can also activate persuasion knowledge or normalize the wrong behavior.

Use only verifiable customers, reviews, ratings, adoption figures, or examples relevant to the audience. Never write “everyone struggles with X” when that normalizes an undesirable behavior. Do not display popularity beside an aggressive CTA by default; test the combination.

### Scarcity and urgency

Meta-analyses find scarcity can affect purchase intention, with results moderated by product and scarcity type. Other evidence shows it can hurt at some stages of a purchase journey.

Mention a deadline, seat limit, batch size, stock count, or capacity only when it is operationally real and will not reset deceptively. Explain what ends and when. If the offer remains available, remove the scarcity line.

### Gain and loss framing

Loss framing is not universally more persuasive. Domain-specific meta-analysis shows the direction can change by behavior and outcome. The often repeated “losses are twice as powerful” figure comes from decision paradigms; it is not an advertising effect size.

Use gain or loss as a test hypothesis. Do not intensify a loss until it becomes fearmongering, a guarantee, or a misleading counterfactual.

### Fear

Fear appeals show a positive average effect in a broad meta-analysis, especially when an effective, attainable response is present. This does not override law, platform policy, brand trust, or the risk of exploiting vulnerable people.

Prefer a factual risk plus a clear remedy. Avoid graphic, catastrophic, guilt-based, or identity-based pressure. Indonesian advertising ethics place additional restrictions on playing on fear.

### Warmth and competence

In customer-service conversations, six studies found value in opening warmly, resolving the issue competently, and closing warmly. This is evidence for interaction sequencing, not a template for every ad.

Use it for sales chat, support, WhatsApp, or service recovery. Do not let warmth delay the answer or let informality substitute for competence.

### Risk reversal

Clear return, trial, cancellation, or guarantee terms can reduce uncertainty. Policy leniency changes both purchase and return behavior, and different dimensions have different effects.

State the real period, scope, exclusions, process, and timing. “30-day refund after one email” is useful only when the business will honor it exactly.

### Curiosity

Curiosity gaps show mixed and nonlinear results. Too little information creates confusion; too much can remove the reason to continue. Platform rules may also reject sensational clickbait.

Leave only the information gap the next click genuinely resolves. The reader should still understand the topic, relevance, and offer before clicking.

### Cultural adaptation

Adapting appeals to broad cultural values has a small average benefit and limited generalization in meta-analysis. Nationality is not a customer insight.

Use actual language, behavior, category expectations, and audience research. Treat culture frameworks as prompts for investigation, never as stereotypes.

## Popular claims to reject

- Do not claim that loss-framed copy is always twice as strong as gain-framed copy.
- Do not claim that emotional copy always beats rational copy.
- Do not assume a hard imperative always improves a CTA.
- Do not assume a curiosity gap, question headline, odd number, “power word,” or the word `you` always wins.
- Do not assume more detail always persuades; irrelevant detail hurts, and over-specific headlines can lose clicks.
- Do not assume social proof or scarcity always helps.
- Do not assume fewer choices always convert better; the average choice-overload effect is near zero and highly contextual.
- Do not describe AIDA, PAS, BAB, 4Ps, or awareness stages as validated universal sequences. Use them as drafting or diagnosis aids.
- Do not treat a testimonial as independent substantiation. The testimonial itself makes a claim.
- Do not turn a correlation, intention measure, case study, or platform engagement metric into a causal sales promise.

## Claim and ethics gate

Before publishing, inspect the net impression, not isolated sentences.

- Require prior evidence for every objective express or implied claim.
- Keep qualifications clear, close, and prominent enough to be noticed.
- Disclose sponsorship, affiliate, employment, or other material connections.
- Never create, buy, suppress, rewrite, or selectively present reviews in a misleading way.
- Never fabricate countdowns, inventory, queues, visitors, endorsements, certifications, comparisons, or reference prices.
- Show total costs, renewal, auto-charge, cancellation, and material eligibility terms before commitment.
- Check the current law and platform policy in the target market. The references below are a baseline, not legal advice or an exhaustive global rule set.

## Testing discipline

Create a control and variants with one main difference each. Write the hypothesis before the result, for example:

- `Specific mechanism will improve qualified landing-page visits over a generic benefit.`
- `Verifiable peer proof will improve completed demos over an unsupported authority claim.`
- `A low-friction CTA will improve qualified replies over a commanding CTA.`

Choose the closest business outcome available: profit or retained revenue, completed sale, qualified lead, activation, then click or engagement only when nothing closer is measurable. Monitor harmful side effects such as refunds, complaints, unsubscribes, low-quality leads, or policy rejection.

Do not declare a winner from small samples, unequal delivery, post-hoc segments, or observational attribution alone.
